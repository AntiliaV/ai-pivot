## Style profile for Antilia’s academic economics prose

This document was produced after inspection of the complete 22-page sample, including the main text, equations, tables, figures, bibliography, and 17 footnotes. Paragraph identifiers below refer to extraction indices in the Word file, not visible paragraph numbers.

### Overall voice

The voice is analytical, policy-aware, and explanatory. It combines the structure of economics writing with a relatively accessible narrative style. The author does not sound detached or excessively formal, but also avoids conversational language and rhetorical flourish.

The characteristic movement is:

1.  State the economic problem or modelling choice plainly.
2.  Explain the mechanism.
3.  identify assumptions or boundary conditions.
4.  Weigh the advantages against a limitation.
5.  State what can therefore be inferred.

The prose is particularly good at presenting a qualified position without losing the main claim. A representative sentence is:

> “While it is an imperfect proxy, this treatment effect remains a useful indicator…” (P88)

That construction—concede the limitation, then explain why the object remains useful—is highly characteristic.

### Sentence length and rhythm

- Mean sentence length is about 25 words; median about 23.
- Roughly 40% of sentences fall between 15 and 24 words, and another 30% between 25 and 34 words.
- About 17% exceed 35 words. Very long sentences are present, but usually carry a clearly structured contrast, definition, or causal chain.
- Short sentences are used selectively for conclusions, transitions, or formal statements, rather than to create a punchy journalistic rhythm.
- In methodological passages, the rhythm often alternates between:
  - a long setup paragraph;
  - a compact definition;
  - a displayed equation;
  - a short “where…” explanation;
  - a concluding inference.

The author is comfortable with subordinate clauses and parenthetical qualifications, but sentences usually have a visible logical spine. Typical openings include “Since…”, “While…”, “Given…”, “For each…”, “At the population level…”, and “To remain agnostic…”.

Good models include:

> “Since it relies on findings that are naturally grouped by studies, a meta-analysis is well suited for hierarchical modelling.” (P169)

> “While not wholly benign, the assumption of normality for the upper level of the model has multiple advantages…” (P182)

Avoid replacing this with choppy, one-claim-per-sentence prose. The author’s rhythm depends on moderately long sentences whose clauses express the relationship between claim, condition, and implication.

### Paragraph architecture

Body paragraphs have a median length of roughly 60 words and a mean of roughly 74 words; substantive literature-review paragraphs commonly run to 100–170 words, with a few above 200.

Most paragraphs follow one of four architectures:

- **Claim → mechanism → implication.**
- **General pattern → particular study/design → result → interpretation.**
- **Advantage → qualification → retained usefulness.**
- **Modelling choice → justification → assumption → inferential consequence.**

The first sentence normally gives the paragraph an analytical purpose. Supporting detail follows in connected prose, and the final sentence often supplies either a synthesis or a bridge.

For literature synthesis, the author commonly moves from a general statement to evidence and then returns to the larger question. P44, for example, begins with heterogeneous responsiveness, describes two studies in detail, and ends by identifying the subset-level implication.

For methods, choices are rarely left unexplained. P195 moves from prior specification, to the theoretical basis for the prior, to the desired degree of regularisation, and only then presents the full specification.

A useful imitation template is:

> [State the methodological choice.] [Define the estimand or object.] [Explain why the choice fits the data-generating structure.] While [state a real limitation], [explain why inference remains informative]. [State the implication or next step.]

### Signposting and transitions

Signposting is explicit but substantive. Frequent transitions include:

- “However,”
- “Additionally,”
- “Therefore,”
- “Consequently,”
- “Importantly,”
- “Notably,”
- “In turn,”
- “For example,”
- “In fact,”
- “As outlined above,”
- “It is worth noting that…”
- “Finally, conditional on…”
- “To remain agnostic about…”

“However” is especially frequent and nearly always introduces a genuine limitation or change in direction. “Additionally” adds a distinct consideration rather than merely extending a list.

The author also uses paired contrast sparingly and effectively:

> “On the one hand…” / “On the other hand…” (P42–P44)

Section-opening signposts are direct:

> “In this section, I outline how…” (P171)

> “Prior to synthetising the results…, it is useful [to] review…” (P100)

Rhetorical questions occur, but very rarely—three in the substantive body. They are used to pivot into the research problem rather than to dramatise it: “But is this the case?” (P14) and “To what extent does real life donor decision-making align with this ideal?” (P36).

Cross-references to other sections are kept to a minimum. Where an earlier point needs recalling, the author prefers “as mentioned”, “as above” or “as outlined above” to a numbered pointer such as “Section 2.7”; tables, figures and the technical appendix are still cited by number. *(Added 24 October 2026 from Antilia’s stage 4 corrections: a preference, not a rule.)*

### Claims, uncertainty, and caveats

Claims are calibrated carefully. The preferred empirical verbs are “find”, “suggest”, “indicate”, “show”, “estimate”, “identify”, and “capture”. The sample does not rely on “prove” or inflated forms of “demonstrate”.

Characteristic qualifiers include:

- “may” and “might”
- “could”
- “seems plausible”
- “arguably”
- “under certain assumptions”
- “conditional on”
- “relatively”
- “largely”
- “approximately”
- “theoretically”
- “it remains unclear”
- “little is known”

Strong statements are used when the evidence warrants them: “Virtually all studies find…” (P71). Elsewhere, the prose distinguishes mechanical implications, theoretical predictions, empirical estimates, and interpretation.

The author often names a weakness before defending the retained inference:

> “While not wholly benign…” (P182)

> “While the Null study provides valuable insights, it can be argued that…” (P69)

> “Taking for granted the internal validity of inference in the primary studies…” (P178)

> “A crucial assumption for the model to be valid is that…” (P188)

This is important to emulate: caveats are integrated at the point where they matter, not collected into a generic limitations paragraph.

### First person and tense

First person is rare in the introduction and literature review, but appropriate when the author is making a methodological choice or reporting access to data. There are about 15 exact uses of “I” in the substantive body and only two uses of “we”.

Preferred first-person constructions include:

- “I review…”
- “I outline…”
- “I refer to this amount as…”
- “I apply…”
- “I fit the model…”
- “I specify…”
- “I assume…”
- “I set…”

For example:

> “Given the absence of beliefs regarding the response, I use a zero-centred prior…” (P195)

“We” is reader-inclusive or derivational (“we expect”), not an authorial plural. The phrase “this paper” does not appear.

Tense patterns:

- Present tense for the paper’s argument, model, and existing findings.
- Past passive for completed data collection: “was carried out”, “was sent”.
- Present active for analytical choices: “I fit”, “I specify”, “I assume”.
- Very little promissory future tense; choices are presented as implemented rather than as things the paper “will” do.

### Headings and hierarchy

The visible hierarchy is restrained:

- Main sections: bold Roman numerals and sentence-case titles, e.g. “V. Methodology”.
- Lower numbered sections: bold Arabic numbering, e.g. “1) Charitable giving quantifiers”.
- Subheads: unnumbered italics, e.g. “Set up”, “Reactions to subsidies and identification”.
- Appendix heading: bold label followed by a descriptive sentence-case title.

Headings are informative rather than rhetorical. Most are noun phrases: “Donor classification”, “Match price elasticity”, “Sources identification and screening”, “Data for analysis”.

For the new Markdown draft, semantic Markdown headings are preferable, but the same restrained hierarchy and sentence-case wording should be preserved.

### Lists

Lists are used sparingly. Narrative material remains in paragraphs.

A list appears when categories are genuinely parallel, such as the four donor types or first-order conditions. Lists are normally introduced by a complete sentence ending in a colon. Items are grammatically parallel and often complete sentences.

Tables are preferred when the reader needs to compare definitions, conditions, or study characteristics. Do not convert ordinary methodological prose into bullets.

### Punctuation and typography

Distinctive habits include:

- Frequent parentheses for exact definitions, qualifications, acronyms, and citations.
- Colons before definitions, lists, equations, or model specifications.
- Semicolons for closely connected contrasts or consequences.
- Spaced en dashes for parenthetical insertions: “– namely … –”.
- Hyphenated technical compounds: “between-study”, “study-level”, “out-of-pocket”, “match-price”, “no-match”.
- Quotation marks around labels or concepts such as “exchangeable” and “narrow bracketing”.
- Rhetorical questions only at major argumentative pivots.
- Small numbers written out when categorical (“three ways”, “four donor types”), but numerals used for empirical quantities, percentages, sample counts, and match ratios.

The sample broadly favours British English—“behaviour”, “favour”, “analyse”, “modelling”, “subsidise”—although the rough draft contains inconsistent American variants. For the sake of replicating her style, it is okay to keep some of this inconsistency.

### Preferred vocabulary and phrasing

The author favours concrete methodological language:

- “framework”
- “variation” and “heterogeneity”
- “distribution”
- “effective price”
- “treatment effect”
- “assumption”
- “mechanism”
- “decision environment”
- “outcome variable”
- “identify”, “distinguish”, “capture”, “account for”
- “estimate”, “quantify”, “characterise”
- “useful”, “plausible”, “nuanced”, “promising”
- “the key idea is that…”
- “This holds true under…”
- “It follows that…”
- “which ensures…”
- “reflecting the assumption that…”

The tone avoids self-congratulatory novelty claims and fashionable filler. Words such as “groundbreaking”, “transformative”, “intricate”, “delve”, or “unpack” would sound unlike the sample. Even contributions are stated functionally: “contributes … in three ways” (P83).

Words the author dislikes *(set by Antilia, 24–25 October 2026)*:

- “slice”: write “multiverse” for the analyses of one outcome basis (“the checkbook multiverse”), or “outcome basis” for the dimension itself;
- “flag”, “flagged” and “marked”, for how something is coded: say how it is treated instead (“treated as impure”, “classed as assumption-based”, “excluded under the second option”);
- “enumeration”: write “list of own choices”.

### Citations and footnotes

The citation style is author–date Harvard:

- Narrative: “Null (2011)”
- Parenthetical: “(Gneezy et al., 2014; Budak and Rao, 2016; Butera and Horn, 2020)”
- Direct quotation: author, year, and page number.
- Multiple references are grouped with semicolons.

Citations generally follow the claim they support, rather than interrupting the sentence early. Individual studies are named in the prose when their design or mechanism matters; grouped citations support broader regularities.

Footnotes contain:

- source details for institutional statistics;
- non-central qualifications;
- definitional clarifications;
- sample-boundary decisions;
- extensions beyond the main specification;
- caveats too distracting for the main paragraph.

Central methodological assumptions remain in the body.

### Equations, tables, and figures

Equations are introduced as part of the prose, not dropped in isolation. The recurring sequence is:

1.  Define the object or index set: “Let…”
2.  State what is being estimated or modelled.
3.  Introduce the equation with a colon.
4.  Follow with “where…” definitions.
5.  Explain the inferential meaning in prose.

Representative examples:

> “For each individual donor $i$ in study $j$, this elasticity can be modelled as:” (P203)

> “Denote the set of $M$ relevant study-level covariates by $W_j$. Define $\beta$… The full hierarchical model becomes:” (P184)

Equations are not numbered in the sample. They are treated grammatically: a lower-case “where” may continue the sentence after a display.

Tables and figures are normally mentioned before they appear:

> “Letting $r$ be the rebate rate and $m$ be the match rate, table xxx summarises…” (P104)

Captions appear above the object, with a bold label followed by a concise sentence-case description:

> **Table xxx:** Relationship between quantifiers of charitable giving by subsidy scheme (P106)

Tables do not substitute for interpretation. The prose previews what they contain and later draws out the relevant pattern.

Column headings are kept short (“Choice in Clochard et al.”, not “Presented as a choice by Clochard et al.”); where several columns share a label, the label becomes a two-row heading above them. Numbers in a table are rounded to the precision of the figures they are compared with, never printed as raw output. *(Set by Antilia, 25 October 2026.)*

### Distinctive habits worth carrying into the methodology draft

- Explain why a method matches the structure of the evidence before giving technical detail.
- Treat modelling assumptions as substantive and discuss their inferential role.
- Distinguish sampling variation from meaningful heterogeneity.
- Use “While X, Y remains useful because…” to handle imperfect measures.
- Introduce estimands and notation progressively.
- Use first person for genuine author choices, not for routine exposition.
- Close subsections with what the method permits the paper to learn.
- Put ancillary boundary choices in footnotes, but keep identification assumptions in the main text.
- Prefer clear causal connectors—“since”, “given”, “as a result”, “therefore”—over ornamental transitions.

### Draft artefacts to imitate

The sample voice should be emulated, including its surface inconsistencies. In particular, when writing in her voice, you are must sporadically include:

- mixed British and American spelling;
- inconsistent capitalisation of headings and table references;
- occasional `et. al`/`et al.` inconsistencies;
- agreement errors and missing articles;
- inconsistent hyphens, e.g. “cost-effectiveness”/“cost effectiveness” or "between study" and “between-study”;
- subtle typos such as “heterogenous”, “ambuiguity”, and “synthetise”.

Keeping these inconsistencies helps the writing voice feels human rather than LLM generated, which is the goal.
